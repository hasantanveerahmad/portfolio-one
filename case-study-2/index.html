Order Hang Tags — Refactor

An end-to-end redesign that streamlines how brands and garment manufacturers order LYCRA-branded hang tags.

How the system works

A LYCRA hang tag is the branded label attached to finished garments by brands such as Lululemon or Under Armour. It certifies that the fabric contains genuine LYCRA fiber. To order tags, customers need a Fast Track Code (FTC) showing that their fabric was tested and approved.

Two entry points

Most brands arrive with a code from their certified mill; fewer receive an FTC after completing the fabric-testing flow themselves.

The Problem

Order Hang Tags was the most-used flow on the LYCRA ONE portal, yet users struggled to complete routine orders. The combination of high volume and high friction made the redesign a business priority, as stakeholders consistently heard frustrations from brands using the system.

As the sole UX/UI designer, my challenge was to simplify the experience within strict technical constraints, reducing the flow from seven steps to five while eliminating friction throughout.

Discovery & Research

The Legacy Flow

The legacy flow ran seven interface steps across eight separate page loads. At approximately six seconds per load, page transitions alone added nearly a minute of waiting to a routine order.

Testing with no budget

With no budget for formal usability testing, I conducted guerrilla usability sessions with four participants using realistic test data: a sample FTC, purchase quantities, fabric consumption figures, a shipping address, and shipping method.

I timed each session and observed where participants struggled. Since they weren't actual brand customers, I used their feedback to diagnose layout and interaction issues rather than evaluate industry-specific requirements.

Guerrilla usability sessions and remote testing of the legacy workflow.

What I found

Hidden field dependencies

Garment Segment and Product Type appeared unrelated, leaving users unaware that one depended on the other.

Delayed validation

Errors appeared only after a full page reload, so users discovered mistakes after a six-second wait.

Conflicting shipping options

Users could select free shipping alongside a paid carrier, even if their address was ineligible.

Dead-end tag eligibility

Tag availability depended on information entered across several steps, leaving some users with no valid tags at the end.

The diagnosis

The legacy flow failed in two fundamental ways:

1. State management

Fields didn't communicate their dependencies, allowing conflicting or invalid selections.

2. Feedback timing

Errors surfaced only after a six-second page reload instead of being prevented or caught immediately.

Design & solution

The redesign reduced seven steps to five, rebuilding every step visually and structurally.

The single biggest constraint

The platform couldn't save progress, so orders had to be completed in one session. If users closed the tab, timed out, or stepped away to find a missing specification, the order was lost and they had to restart.

Supporting save-and-resume meant rebuilding the backend, which was outside the project scope. The challenge was to make a single-session flow faster and more forgiving, without changing the underlying architecture.

How I designed around it
  • Front-load requirements. A Welcome popup explains what users need before starting.
  • Reuse saved information. Saved FTCs and addresses reduce repetitive work and interruptions.
  • Preserve flexibility. Back-navigation and in-session editing let users fix earlier selections without restarting.

Working through the complexity

Over several months, Enrique (business requirements), Sohail (CMS and data constraints), and I worked through every step on a shared Miro board. We mapped legacy screens, debated which fields to keep, remove, or combine, and explored user states before finalizing the structure.

7 steps, cut down to 5

Two consolidations removed full page loads: Welcome became a popup, and brand selection was merged with hang tag selection. Fabric Details also moved to the beginning so the required FTC comes first.

Step 0/1: Welcome & Fabric Details

Every order required an FTC, but the legacy flow asked for Garment Details first and buried fabric verification two steps later. I reordered the experience around this dependency and made it easier for returning users to reuse a code.

Remove an unnecessary page load

The legacy Welcome page collected no input. I moved the same instructions into a popup over Fabric Details, so users could continue directly into the first step.

Recognition over recall

Returning users previously had to retrieve and retype a seven-character FTC. I added a saved-code list alongside the option to enter a new code, prioritizing the most common returning-user path.

Fabric details are still required

Selecting a saved FTC confirms eligibility, but users must still enter purchase quantities and consumption details for their current order.

Step 2: Garment Details

The legacy form made related fields look independent. In usability testing, 3 of 4 participants didn't realize Product Type depended on the Garment Segment selected earlier.

What changed

I divided the form into three sections, replaced the Segment dropdown with an icon-based Category selector, and filtered Product Type choices by the selected category. Helper text reinforces the dependency.

How I arrived at it

I mapped the garment taxonomy alongside the interface and worked with the PM and CMS lead to validate dependencies, refine terminology, and account for engineering constraints.

Step 3: Shipping

In testing, users selected Free Shipping alongside a paid carrier, or chose it for an address that didn't qualify. The legacy screen presented conflicting options and only surfaced errors after a reload.

Design decision: Prevent invalid states

I mapped saved and new address paths with the team, then combined shipping methods into one radio group and tied free-shipping eligibility to the selected address. Invalid combinations are no longer presented, preventing errors before submission.

Step 4: Hang Tag Selection

The legacy flow separated brand selection and hang tag selection into two pages. Users selected a brand, waited through a reload, then saw available tags without a visible connection to their previous choice.

Design decision: Make dependencies visible

I merged the two pages into one step so users could see how their brand choice determines the available tags. This eliminated a page load and clarified cause and effect. A custom-tag passcode field also appears only when applicable.

Steps 5 & 6: Review & Confirmation

The legacy Review step hid shipping details behind dropdowns, while Confirmation instructed users to print and mail their order email with a fabric sample. I made shipping details visible during review and redesigned Confirmation to clarify what happens after submission.

Design decision: Turn confirmation into a feedback opportunity

I worked with the team to improve order visibility, clarify next steps, and introduce an optional satisfaction survey. This created a way to evaluate the experience after launch, ultimately collecting 623 responses over eight months.

Task efficiency

I repeated the same moderated protocol with the original four participants. Average completion time dropped from 7:48 to 4:15, a 45% improvement. Friction points that had blocked participants on the legacy version no longer stopped them.

With only four participants, this is a directional finding. The reduction reflects both the simplified workflow and faster page loads, rather than design changes alone.

Post-launch satisfaction

The optional survey on the Confirmation page let customers rate their experience Good, Neutral, or Poor and leave comments. Over eight months it collected 623 responses: 548 Good, 58 Neutral, and 17 Poor.

The survey appeared after submission and participation was voluntary. Results therefore represent completed orders and self-selected respondents, not everyone who attempted to order.

What users said

Comments frequently praised speed, simplicity, and ease of use.

“Fast, simple, and easy to use, a great experience.”

“The new system is smooth, intuitive, and done in one go.”

“The ease of ordering they implemented is fantastic.”

What the feedback didn't tell us

An 88% positive rating does not mean every issue was resolved. I categorized complaints to separate friction within my redesigned flow from problems elsewhere in the portal.

Five of the seven recurring complaint themes concerned broader portal issues, including file uploads, login, performance, support responsiveness, and language support. Two themes deserve closer examination.

Post-submission order changes were constrained by backend order management, not by the in-session flow. Saved-address selection, however, was part of this redesign and remained a source of friction. I would test and improve that interaction next.

Potential business impact, not measured

Faster completion and preventing invalid choices may have reduced abandoned orders, support contacts, and incorrect orders reaching fulfillment. I did not have access to support-ticket or fulfillment data to verify these outcomes.

Reflection & Next Steps

Product next step

Users could navigate back and edit any step before submission, but they couldn't modify an order once it entered processing. The clearest future opportunity is a post-submission order-management capability, allowing customers to amend or cancel orders without contacting support.

What I learned

Some constraints simply exist, and good design works within them. Save-and-resume required backend work the project couldn't absorb. Instead, I made the single-session flow more resilient by front-loading requirements, reusing saved information, and allowing in-session edits.

The same principle applied to scope. The brand logos on Hang Tag Selection weren't my preferred visual treatment, but they worked and more important usability problems needed attention. Knowing what not to redesign is a design decision too.

I also learned to align on scope with every relevant team earlier. A Garment Retail Region section was absent from the original scope, but the team operating the Salesforce data later identified it as useful. Adding it late cost avoidable development hours. I now treat upfront alignment across all data owners as part of discovery.
